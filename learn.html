1. The 5-Year Threshold for Permanent Employees is Unchanged

A common misconception is that the "1-year rule" applies to everyone.

The caveat: The 1-year eligibility rule is strictly for Fixed-Term Employees (FTEs) who are on contractual roles. Permanent staff on the company payroll still require 5 years of continuous service to be eligible for gratuity.
Left before completing 5 years (permanent) or 1 year (fixed-term)? No statutory gratuity — the payout can be ₹0. But read your payslips and appointment letter before signing your Full & Final (F&F): a gratuity line inside the CTC is normally just the employer's provisioning cost (≈4.81% of basic p.a.), not a deduction from your cash salary — there is nothing to refund in that case. However, if the company actually deducted a monthly amount labelled "gratuity" from your earnings, that is an unlawful deduction — the Payment of Gratuity Act does not allow gratuity to be funded from an employee's salary — and you can claim it back in your F&F settlement (as a wrongful salary deduction, not as gratuity).

Action plan: (1) Scan monthly payslips for a "Gratuity" line under Deductions; (2) re-read the appointment/offer letter for whether gratuity is "deducted monthly" or only a company retirement benefit; (3) if actually deducted, email HR: "Since I am leaving before the vesting period, please refund all monthly gratuity components deducted from my CTC as part of my Full & Final settlement."

2. Private School Teachers and Staff are Covered

Gratuity is not only for corporate employees.

The caveat: Following landmark legal precedents, teachers and staff in private schools and educational institutions are fully eligible for statutory gratuity on par with employees in any other covered organization. Being a school does not exempt the employer from the gratuity rules.

3. Contract Employees via Third Parties are Excluded

Not all contract workers benefit from the new 1-year timeline.

The caveat: If you are a contractual worker hired through a third-party staffing agency or contractor to work at a client company, you do not qualify for the 1-year direct gratuity rule from that client company. Your eligibility depends on your employment tenure with your actual employer (the staffing agency).

4. The "4 Years and 7 Months" Gray Area (The 240-Day Rule)

The law specifies "continuous service" — but courts interpret a "completed year" differently based on active working days.

The caveat: For a regular 5-day or 6-day week office, completing 240 working days in the final year of service counts as a full year. So a permanent employee who resigns after 4 years and 240 days (roughly 4 years and 7–8 months) is technically eligible under settled judicial precedents — though some employers still rigidly enforce a strict 5-calendar-year rule.
Example: You joined on 1 Jan 2021 and resign on 1 Aug 2025. That is ~4 years 7 months with ~240+ working days in the final year. Under the 240-day rule you may qualify — but be prepared to push back if HR quotes the strict 5-year rule.

5. Total Forfeiture for Misconduct

Gratuity is a reward for long-term loyalty — but it can be legally withheld.

The caveat: An employer can completely or partially forfeit your gratuity if your services are terminated due to riotous or disorderly conduct, acts of violence, moral turpitude, or if you cause willful damage/loss to the company's property. The company must follow proper disciplinary procedures and issue a formal forfeiture notice.
If you are fired for serious misconduct (e.g. violence at work), the gratuity may not be paid. This is different from a routine resignation or a reduction in force.

6. The 10-Employee Mandate — and "Once Covered, Always Covered"

The updated social security codes do not automatically apply to every business.

The mandate: An organization is legally mandated to pay gratuity only if it has employed 10 or more people on any single day in the preceding 12 months. If a startup or small business operates with fewer than 10 employees, they are exempt from paying statutory gratuity (though an employer may still voluntarily pay more).
  • Once covered, always covered: if a company's headcount later drops below 10, it cannot stop providing gratuity — it remains legally bound by the Act permanently.

7. The ₹20 Lakh Tax Exemption Cap Includes Past Payouts

The maximum tax-free gratuity is ₹20,00,000 for private-sector employees.

The caveat: This cap is a lifetime cumulative limit. If you switch jobs and receive ₹12 lakh tax-free gratuity from Company A, then later receive ₹15 lakh from Company B, only ₹8 lakh of the second payout is tax-exempt. The remaining ₹7 lakh is added to your taxable income.
  • Government employees: gratuity is 100% tax-free with no upper limit.
  • Private sector & PSUs: the tax-exempt amount for a company covered by the Act is the lowest of: the actual gratuity received, the statutory ₹20 lakh cap, and the amount derived from the 15/26 formula. Since the calculator already uses the 15/26 formula, this works out to the cap (₹20,00,000 minus any prior gratuity).
Company A pays ₹12 L (tax-free, uses ₹12 L of the ₹20 L ceiling). Remaining ceiling = ₹8 L. Company B pays ₹15 L → ₹8 L exempt, ₹7 L taxable.

Companies Covered Under the Act vs Not Covered — the Formula Difference

The total gratuity calculation can differ significantly depending on whether your organization is formally covered by the Payment of Gratuity Act or pays gratuity voluntarily.

What counts as salary Basic Pay + DA (must be ≥ 50% of CTC) Basic Pay + DA (varies by contract terms)

The calculator on the home page uses the covered-companies formula (15/26), which is the statutory default. If your employer is not covered but pays gratuity voluntarily, the actual payment may follow the 15/30 formula or your contract, so check with HR.

Delays, Interest, and Legal Protections

The law protects employees from delays and preserves the gratuity amount from creditors.

  • Interest on delays: if the employer delays payment beyond the 30-day window without a valid reason, it must pay simple interest (typically around 10% per annum) from the day the gratuity became due until the actual payment date.
  • Legally protected from attachment: gratuity cannot be attached by any court order or debt-recovery agency to pay off an employee's personal loans or bankruptcy dues.

Landmark Court Judgements on Gratuity

Following the legal enforcement of the Code on Social Security, the Supreme Court of India and various High Courts have delivered judgements clarifying employer liability, employee misconduct, and jurisdictional crossovers.

1. Prabhat Kumar Singh & Others v. Accu Pack — August 2026

Core ruling: No withholding or forfeiting gratuity based on unproven, generic claims of financial loss.

The case: The employer attempted to forfeit a large amount of employee gratuity (citing a ₹50 lakh loss) under Section 4(6)(a) after terminating employees for alleged misconduct.

The judgement: The Supreme Court held that while employers may forfeit gratuity for damage or loss caused by an employee's misconduct, the forfeiture must strictly equal the exact amount of proven financial loss. The deduction was rejected because the company failed to produce a verified audit report or independent accounting evidence substantiating the loss.

2. N. Manoharan v. The Administrative Officer & Anr. — February 2026

Core ruling: Government civil servants are strictly excluded from the Payment of Gratuity Act (PGA).

The case: Retired employees of the Heavy Water Plant, Tuticorin (Department of Atomic Energy) sought gratuity under the standard corporate PGA formula rather than government service limits.

The judgement: The Supreme Court clarified that Central Government civil servants holding civil posts are governed exclusively by the Central Civil Services (CCS) Pension Rules and cannot parallelly file claims under the PGA.

3. Western Coal Fields Ltd. v. Manohar Govinda Fulzele — Late 2025/2026

Core ruling: Criminal conviction is not mandatory to forfeit gratuity on grounds of moral turpitude.

The case: This ruling overturned the earlier Union Bank of India v. C.G. Ajay Babu precedent (2018), which required a criminal conviction before gratuity could be forfeited.

The judgement: An internal disciplinary inquiry held with due process (natural justice) is sufficient to validate a forfeiture for acts of violence or moral turpitude — the employer need not wait for a criminal court verdict.

4. High Court Mandates on the 10% Statutory Delay Interest

Core ruling: Internal administrative or management disputes are invalid excuses to delay employee payouts.

The judgement: In multi-bench judgements (including the Bombay High Court), the courts have tightly enforced the 30-day settlement window. Even where a company undergoes a management overhaul, board split, or internal disputes, employee statutory rights remain independent. If gratuity is unpaid after 30 days, employers must pay 10% per annum simple interest calculated from the day the retirement/resignation took effect.

Related legislative update: To ensure consistent enforcement of the new social-security code across all states, the Supreme Court has directed the Union Government to streamline strict operational rules so that parallel provisions (including benefit timelines under the code) are implemented uniformly. The practical effect for employees is stronger, faster settlement of statutory benefits such as gratuity.

Nominations and Death Benefits

Employees are expected to complete a Form F (Nomination Form) once they complete a year of service.

  • In the event of death: the gratuity becomes immediately payable to the designated nominee or legal heirs — the tenure requirements are waived (see General Exemptions below).
  • Minor nominees: if the nominee is a minor at the time of payout, the employer must deposit the cash into a bank savings account or a controlled fund that is released only when the minor turns 18.

General Exemptions — When the 5-Year Rule is Waived Entirely

The only scenarios where a permanent employee does not need to meet the 5-year (or 4-year-240-day) requirement are:

  • Death of the employee — the payout goes to the nominee or legal heirs.
  • Permanent disablement caused by an accident or occupational disease during employment.

2025 Key Facts

  • Effective date: Updated social security provisions came into force on November 21, 2025.
  • Payment timeline: Employers must settle gratuity within 30 days of it becoming payable.
  • Tax exemption limit: Maximum tax-free gratuity remains ₹20 lakh for private-sector employees.
  • Fixed-term employees: Qualify after 1 year of continuous service on a pro-rata basis.
  • 50% wage rule: Basic wages must be at least 50% of CTC — allowances above that get added back to the gratuity base.
  • Form I: Claims are submitted via Form I; you must declare prior gratuity received.
  • Surcharge on high incomes: When your total income (including the taxable gratuity) crosses ₹50 lakh / ₹1 crore / ₹2 crore / ₹5 crore, a surcharge of 10% / 15% / 25% / 37% is added on the income-tax amount — the calculator shows this automatically.
