Key rules, caveats, and myths under the Code on Social Security, 2025

1. The 5-Year Threshold for Permanent Employees is Unchanged

A common misconception is that the "1-year rule" applies to everyone.

The caveat: The 1-year eligibility rule is strictly for Fixed-Term Employees (FTEs) who are on contractual roles. Permanent staff on the company payroll still require 5 years of continuous service to be eligible for gratuity.

2. Contract Employees via Third Parties are Excluded

Not all contract workers benefit from the new 1-year timeline.

The caveat: If you are a contractual worker hired through a third-party staffing agency or contractor to work at a client company, you do not qualify for the 1-year direct gratuity rule from that client company. Your eligibility depends on your employment tenure with your actual employer (the staffing agency).

3. The "4 Years and 7 Months" Gray Area (The 240-Day Rule)

The law specifies "continuous service" — but courts interpret a "completed year" differently based on active working days.

The caveat: For a regular 5-day or 6-day week office, completing 240 working days in the final year of service counts as a full year. So a permanent employee who resigns after 4 years and 240 days (roughly 4 years and 7–8 months) is technically eligible under settled judicial precedents — though some employers still rigidly enforce a strict 5-calendar-year rule.
Example: You joined on 1 Jan 2021 and resign on 1 Aug 2025. That is ~4 years 7 months with ~240+ working days in the final year. Under the 240-day rule you may qualify — but be prepared to push back if HR quotes the strict 5-year rule.

4. Total Forfeiture for Misconduct

Gratuity is a reward for long-term loyalty — but it can be legally withheld.

The caveat: An employer can completely or partially forfeit your gratuity if your services are terminated due to riotous or disorderly conduct, acts of violence, moral turpitude, or if you cause willful damage/loss to the company's property. The company must follow proper disciplinary procedures and issue a formal forfeiture notice.
If you are fired for serious misconduct (e.g. violence at work), the gratuity may not be paid. This is different from a routine resignation or a reduction in force.

5. Employer Size Restrictions (The 10-Employee Rule)

The updated social security codes do not automatically apply to every business.

The caveat: An organization is legally mandated to pay gratuity only if it has employed 10 or more people on any single day in the preceding 12 months. If a startup or small business operates with fewer than 10 employees, they are exempt from paying statutory gratuity (though an employer may still voluntarily pay more).

6. The ₹20 Lakh Tax Exemption Cap Includes Past Payouts

The maximum tax-free gratuity is ₹20,00,000 for private-sector employees.

The caveat: This cap is a lifetime cumulative limit. If you switch jobs and receive ₹12 lakh tax-free gratuity from Company A, then later receive ₹15 lakh from Company B, only ₹8 lakh of the second payout is tax-exempt. The remaining ₹7 lakh is added to your taxable income.
Company A pays ₹12 L (tax-free, uses ₹12 L of the ₹20 L ceiling). Remaining ceiling = ₹8 L. Company B pays ₹15 L → ₹8 L exempt, ₹7 L taxable.

General Exemptions — When the 5-Year Rule is Waived Entirely

The only scenarios where a permanent employee does not need to meet the 5-year (or 4-year-240-day) requirement are:

  • Death of the employee — the payout goes to the nominee or legal heirs.
  • Permanent disablement caused by an accident or occupational disease during employment.

2025 Key Facts

  • Effective date: Updated social security provisions came into force on November 21, 2025.
  • Payment timeline: Employers must settle gratuity within 30 days of it becoming payable.
  • Tax exemption limit: Maximum tax-free gratuity remains ₹20 lakh for private-sector employees.
  • Fixed-term employees: Qualify after 1 year of continuous service on a pro-rata basis.
  • 50% wage rule: Basic wages must be at least 50% of CTC — allowances above that get added back to the gratuity base.
  • Form I: Claims are submitted via Form I; you must declare prior gratuity received.
